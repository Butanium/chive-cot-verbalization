Qwen3-8B reasoning names the verified cause only 10.7 points more often than an inert factor

The chain of thought attributes a behavior to its verified causal factor in 42.0% of behavior-exhibiting completions (95% CI 34.2–50.0%), but an inert factor from the same conversation receives 32.6% (CI 24.5–40.9%), and 40.6% when the inert edit had exactly zero effect. The plan pre-registered that a control this far from zero makes the absolute rate uninterpretable as a verbalization rate, so the supportable result is the paired one: attribution to the verified cause exceeds attribution to an inert factor by 10.7 percentage points (CI 2.4–19.2) over 87 pairs, and by 2.9 points (CI -13.9–18.5) on the 26 zero-effect pairs. The 42.0% is an upper bound on how often the trace verbalizes the verified cause. This is a partial signal.

This study extends Would this change your answer? Evaluating Explanations of LLM Behavior In The Wild with Counterfactual Experiments. We resampled 100 released Qwen3-8B reasoning-mode investigations and judged whether each behavior-exhibiting trace named the paper’s counterfactually verified cause as a reason.

Main takeaways

  • Verbalized reasons discriminate the verified cause from an inert factor only weakly: the paired advantage is 10.7 points pooled and near zero on inert factors with exactly zero effect. Causal and inert per-investigation rates correlate at r = 0.45, and the inert rate is at least as high as the causal rate in 43 of 87 pairs. See the paired comparison.
  • The raw 42.0% causal attribution rate is an upper bound, not a verbalization rate: 49.0% of behavior samples give a different reason, and the elevated control mixes same-span inert edits, judge false positives, and traces that cite a non-causal reason. See the investigation-level distribution.

Attribution varies across investigations

The pooled 42.0% rate covers behavior-exhibiting completions from 100 investigations. The median investigation-level rate is 35.0%, and the distribution spans the full range.

Attribution is not a stable property of reasoning mode across investigations: 22% of investigations never name the verified cause, while 15% always do. The inert distribution sits lower (median 0.13; 33% of the 87 paired investigations at zero, 7% at one), but the two tails are not clean: 3 of the 15 “always” investigations (prompt_001863, prompt_002095, prompt_004773) also attribute to their inert factor in every sample, and 15 investigations are at zero for both factors. The inert control limits the interpretation

The inert factor is not a “nothing” control, and the pre-registered rule says a control this far from zero makes the absolute rate uninterpretable. Pooled inert attribution is 32.6%; on the 26 investigations whose inert edit had exactly 0 pp effect it is 40.6% (CI 25.6–56.2%) against 42.1% for the causal factor on the same investigations, a paired difference of 2.9 points (CI -13.9–18.5). Across all 87 pairs the causal and inert per-investigation rates correlate at r = 0.45, and the inert rate is at least as high as the causal rate in 43 pairs (20 strictly higher, 23 tied).

The Q1 mention rate is almost identical for causal and inert factors, 65.5% and 65.8%, while Q2 attribution falls to 42.0% and 32.6%, so the judge does separate mention from attribution. The elevated control has three sources that this design cannot separate. First, same-span inert edits: the inert claim is selected by smallest effect only, without requiring a different part of the prompt, and 8 to 14 of the 87 pairs have factor descriptions that overlap heavily with the causal description (content-word Jaccard at least 0.4 or 0.3). In prompt_002095 the causal factor is a first-person pronoun and the inert factor is the content of the same quoted sentence; both score 1.0. The mean paired difference on the high-overlap pairs is 3.7 to 7.3 points against 11.3 points on the rest. Second, judge false positives: cross-family Q2 agreement on inert behavior items is κ = 0.36, below the 0.4 threshold, with temperature-1 self-agreement 0.60 against 0.82 on causal items. Third, traces that present a non-causal aspect of the prompt as their reason, such as prompt_004403 citing the two-letter input “Nl” as the reason for answering in Spanish.

The corrected judge passes the agreement rule on pooled items, not on inert items

The as-planned judge showed each factor as hypothetical edit text. It estimated 39.8% causal attribution (95% CI 32.0–48.0%) but failed the pre-registered cross-family Q2 rule, κ = 0.370 below 0.4.

The primary corrected judge rewrites each factor into an outcome-blind description of the original conversation content and removes edit framing from the prompt; rationales matching the edit-misreading pattern drop from 680 to 29 of 5610 items. This post-hoc correction passes the rule at κ = 0.468 pooled and moves the headline by 2.2 percentage points, from 39.8% to 42.0%.

Two qualifications apply. The 300 validation items are the same set (seed 42) for both judges, and the v2 defect was diagnosed from Q2 disagreements on these items, so the v3 pass is on the set the redesign was tuned against rather than an independent test. And the pass does not hold on every subset: κ is 0.467 on causal behavior items (n = 124) and 0.529 on non-behavior items (n = 81), but 0.360 on inert behavior items (n = 95). The headline clears the threshold on its own subset; the inert rate and the paired difference rest on judgments below it.

Method

We sampled investigations with large verified single-factor effects, regenerated the original prompts, graded the target behavior, and judged causal-factor attribution only among completions that exhibited it. Rates pool completions, while uncertainty resamples investigations as clusters.

Design

Data
100 investigations sampled from 200 eligible cases; 96 are mistakes and 87 have an inert control.
Selection
Verification score at least 9, absolute effect at least 50 percentage points, and behavior in at least 15 of 30 released samples.
Model
Qwen3-8B revision b968826d, thinking enabled, temperature 1.0, top-p 0.95, and top-k 20.
Sampling
30 completions per prompt, with thinking capped at 1024 tokens and 2048 tokens total.
Outcome
Q2 attribution among 2163 behavior-exhibiting completions, with causal and inert factors judged separately.
Uncertainty
95% intervals from 5000 investigation-cluster bootstrap resamples with seed 42.
Judges
claude-sonnet-5 primary, gpt-5.6-terra validation, and claude-sonnet-4.6 behavior grader.
Serving
SGLang on one H100; generation settings and the 1024-token thinking cap match the released run.

Limitations

  • The inert control is elevated (32.6% pooled, 40.6% for zero-effect inert factors) and mixes same-span inert edits, judge false positives, and verbalized non-causal reasons. The 42.0% rate is an upper bound on verbalizing the verified cause, and the inert rate and paired difference rest partly on judgments with cross-family κ = 0.36, below the pre-registered 0.4.
  • The judge was redesigned after the as-planned prompt failed the agreement check, and the same 300 validation items were used to diagnose the defect and to pass the corrected judge, so the v3 κ of 0.468 is not an independent validation. Both versions are reported; the headline moved by 2.2 points. The second-family judge says yes more often (40% versus 35% on the validation sample), so the absolute rate carries several points of judge-dependent uncertainty.
  • Selection favors large, clean, reproducible effects and includes only four non-mistakes; 21 of 100 investigations reproduced the behavior in fewer than half of fresh samples. The rate may not transfer to subtle behaviors or correct outputs.
  • Slice contrasts (edit type, thinking cap, conversation length, mistake status) all have overlapping intervals and are not distinguishable here.
  • This covers one model and one thinking cap; 26.8% of fresh traces hit the cap.
